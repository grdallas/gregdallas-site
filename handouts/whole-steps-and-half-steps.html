The Major Scale

Whole Steps & Half Steps

The one small thing to memorize about the major scale — and how it shows you where every natural note sits on the guitar neck.

There is a half step between E and F, and a half step between B and C.
Every other step is a whole step.

C major is built from natural notes only — no sharps, no flats — so those are the only two places a half step falls: from the 3rd note to the 4th, and from the 7th up to the 8th. Learn where E–F and B–C sit and the whole scale is spaced for you.

On the keyboard

W is a whole step, H a half step — the nearest note with nothing in between. On a keyboard the half steps are plain to see: E–F and B–C are the only white keys with no black key between them. Every other step skips a black key.

W  W  H  W  W  W  H

Whole, whole, half — whole, whole, whole, half. That spacing is the major scale; keep it from any starting note and you’ve built that note’s major scale.

On the neck

On the guitar a half step is one fret and a whole step is two. Take the open E string: start on E and move up by the same map — H (one fret) only from E–F and B–C, W (two frets) everywhere else. You land on E F G A B C D E, every natural note (the “white keys”) on that string, at frets 0, 1, 3, 5, 7, 8, 10 and 12. Because you start from E rather than C the half steps land in different places — but always between E–F and B–C. Every string works the same way: start from its open note (E A D G B E) and follow the map.

Start on any note other than C and you’ll need a sharp or flat here and there to keep the spacing exact — that is where key signatures come from. (See the diatonic-chords sheet.)
